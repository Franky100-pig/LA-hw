Linear Algebra – Problem Set 4

Vector Spaces & Bases · 12 concept-first problems · Original problems in the spirit of MIT 18.06 (Strang)

Set 3 asked whether particular vectors are independent. This set asks what the words mean. A vector space is an axiomatic generalization of $\mathbb{R}^n$: the only things that matter are the eight axioms, so the same arguments work for polynomials, matrices and functions. A subspace is a vector space sitting inside another one; a basis is a set that is simultaneously minimal (you cannot drop anything) and maximal (you cannot add anything) — those two descriptions are the same fact, and Problem 5 is about proving it. Dimension is what makes the word "basis" safe: it does not matter which basis you pick, the count is the same. Arithmetic is deliberately light here; the object of study is the structure, and the computations are there to keep the definitions honest. Mixed, exam-style problem sets will come later, once there is more machinery to mix.

Problem 1

Test each set for being a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^2$, using the three-step test: (i) does it contain the zero vector, (ii) is it closed under addition, (iii) is it closed under scalar multiplication?

$$S_1 = \left\{\begin{bmatrix} x \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\right\}, \qquad S_2 = \left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ y \end{bmatrix}\right\}, \qquad S_3 = \left\{\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} : xy = 0\right\}, \qquad S_4 = \left\{\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} : x + y = 0\right\}.$$

For each one that fails, name the first condition it fails and give a concrete counterexample. $S_3$ is the instructive one: it is a union of two lines, and it is worth understanding exactly why a union is usually not a subspace.

Problem 2

Which of the following are vector spaces over $\mathbb{R}$? For those that are not, name the first axiom that fails (and, if it is a subspace of something, say what subspace it is).

(a) $\mathbb{R}^2$ with the usual operations.
(b) $\{(x,y) : x > 0\}$.
(c) $\{(x,y) : x + y \geq 0\}$.
(d) $\mathbb{Z}^3$ (integer triples, usual operations).
(e) $\{a + bx + cx^2 : a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}\}$ — polynomials of degree at most $2$.
(f) $\{a + bx + cx^2 : \text{exactly one of } a, b, c \text{ is } \ne 0\}$ — polynomials of degree exactly $2$.

Problem 3

This set is about meaning, so here is a set about why the axioms say what they say. For each axiom below, explain in one or two sentences what it buys you — that is, what would go wrong in the rest of linear algebra if it were dropped.

(a) $0$ belongs to the space, and every $v$ has an additive inverse $-v$.
(b) Addition and scalar multiplication are commutative.
(c) Closure under all real scalars, not just integers.
(d) $1v = v$ (the scalar $1$ is a neutral element for scaling).

Then answer: why is $\{0\}$ a subspace of every vector space, and why is that fact worth having?

Problem 4

Let $S$ be any set of vectors.

(a) Prove that $\operatorname{span}(S)$ is a subspace.
(b) Explain in one sentence why $\operatorname{span}(S)$ is called the smallest subspace containing $S$.
(c) Is every subspace of $\mathbb{R}^3$ spanned by finitely many vectors? Justify your answer with the tools from Set 2 (not with a citation).

Problem 5

Three descriptions of a basis appear in textbooks:

**(i)** a spanning set you cannot shrink; **(ii)** an independent set you cannot extend; **(iii)** a spanning set whose size equals the dimension of the space.

Show that these describe the same thing. The tool is the exchange argument: if $S$ spans a subspace $W$ and $v \in W$ is not in $\operatorname{span}(S)$, then $S$ still spans $W$ after adding $v$, and — this is the part to prove — something must come out.

Then answer: if a set $S$ spans $W$ and no vector of $S$ can be removed without losing the span, must $S$ be independent? (This direction is where students usually assume too much.)

Problem 6

Describe the geometry of the subspaces of $\mathbb{R}^3$ — again, all through the origin.

(a) What do all one-dimensional subspaces look like? Give a parametrization, and say why the line $\{t \cdot v : t \in \mathbb{R}\}$ for a fixed $v$ is the only shape.

(b) Show that $\Pi = \{x + y + z = 0\}$ is a two-dimensional subspace, by exhibiting a basis (and checking it spans).

(c) The set $\Pi' = \{x + y + z = 1\}$ is parallel to $\Pi$ and equally "plane-like". Explain in one line why it is not a subspace.
(d) How many planes through the origin contain a given nonzero vector $v$? (The answer is not "three" — explain why the intuition fails.)

Problem 7

Let

$$U = \operatorname{span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right\}, \qquad V = \operatorname{span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}\right\}.$$

(a) Compute $\dim U$ and $\dim V$.
(b) Find a basis of $U \cap V$.
(c) Find $U + V$ and its dimension.
(d) Verify $\dim(U \cap V) + \dim(U + V) = \dim U + \dim V$, and say what the equality means geometrically.

Problem 8

In Set 3 we used two bases of $\mathbb{R}^3$:

$$B = \left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right\}, \qquad C = \left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right\}.$$

For $w = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$ we found $[w]_B = (1, 0, 2)^T$ and $[w]_C = (1, -1, 2)^T$.

Answer in prose, not arithmetic:

(a) What does a basis buy us that a bare list of vectors does not?
(b) "Dimension" counts something about a vector space. What exactly, and what does it not count?
(c) Why can two different bases of the same space disagree about a vector's coordinates while agreeing about how many vectors the basis has?

Problem 9

For each of the following linear maps $\mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$, find a basis of the kernel and a basis of the image, and decide whether the map is injective and whether it is surjective:

$$T_1(x,y) = (x+y,\; x-y), \qquad T_2(x,y) = (x,\; 2y), \qquad T_3(x,y) = (x+y,\; x+y).$$

Then explain, using the two middle maps, why "injective" and "surjective" are different failures: for these three maps, do the two properties ever disagree, and in which direction?

Problem 10

Let $T : \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ be $T(x,y,z) = (x + y,\; y + z)$.

(a) Find a basis of $\ker T$ and a basis of $\operatorname{im} T$.
(b) Verify $\dim \ker T + \dim \operatorname{im} T = 3$.
(c) State the theorem that (b) is an instance of, and explain in two sentences what it means intuitively — in particular, why it says that a linear map can "collapse" some directions but never "create" new ones.

Problem 11

Prove the rank–nullity theorem: for a linear map $T$ from an $n$-dimensional space to any space,

$$\dim \ker T + \dim \operatorname{im} T = n.$$

Use basis bookkeeping rather than matrices: take a basis of $\ker T$, extend it to a basis of the whole domain, and look at the images of the extended vectors.

Problem 12

True or false? For the false ones, give a counterexample; for the ones that depend on a convention, say which convention makes them true and why.

(a) Every subspace contains a basis.
(b) $\dim \operatorname{span}(S) = |S|$ for every set $S$.
(c) $\{0\}$ is a subspace, and its basis is the empty set.
(d) $\operatorname{im} T$ is a subspace of the domain of $T$.
(e) Every linearly independent set is a basis of its own span.
(f) Two bases of the same space must share at least one vector.
